# Salary, Negotiation and Offers

> **Tier:** T2 (working knowledge) · **Related:** [Common HR questions](03-common-hr-questions.md), [Questions to ask](05-questions-to-ask.md), [Tell me about yourself](01-tell-me-about-yourself.md)
> **On your resume:** your current role (Associate Software Engineer at Devntech since November 2025), about a year of professional experience plus internships, and your stacks (Rails, React, TypeScript, React Native, Django) set the market band you negotiate in.

## Quick revision
- **Research a range before the first call:** a walk-away number, a target and a stretch, each with a source. This file deliberately contains no salary figures; markets move, so use current data.
- **"Expected salary?"** Ask for their budgeted range first if you can; otherwise give a researched range whose bottom is a number you would genuinely accept.
- **Compare packages, not base salaries:** benefits, allowances, increments, and take-home pay after tax.
- **Negotiate after the written offer,** politely, with reasons, once or twice; get the final terms in writing.
- **Evaluate the whole offer:** growth, stack, mentorship, stability, probation, notice period, contract type, hours.
- **Remote foreign-currency contracts:** know whether you are an employee or a contractor, how and when you are paid, what taxes apply, and which law governs the contract. Rules change, so verify current guidance.
- **Resign professionally:** offer signed first, written notice, a handover, no burned bridges, experience letter collected.

## 1. Research your range
Good sources, from most to least reliable:
- **Peers at your level** in the same city and stack: ask for ranges, not exact numbers.
- **Seniors and leads you trust:** what their companies pay at your level.
- **Recruiters:** "What is the budgeted band for this role?" is a normal question.
- **Job posts that publish ranges,** especially for the same title and stack.
- **Review sites and community salary surveys:** useful for direction, but self-reported, often outdated and noisy.

Then build three numbers:

| Number | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Walk-away | Below this you decline; it covers your needs and the cost of switching (commute, risk, losing current benefits) |
| Target | What the research says a fair offer is for your skills and this role |
| Stretch | The top of the realistic band; your opening anchor when you are a strong match |

Adjust for the company type (local software house, product company, foreign-funded startup, remote foreign employer), the city, the shift (night shifts usually come with allowances), and how scarce your stack is for that employer.

In Pakistan, salaries are usually discussed as monthly figures in PKR; always confirm whether a number is gross or net, and monthly or annual.

> **Fill in:** your current gross and net monthly salary, your walk-away, target and stretch numbers, and the source behind each.

## 2. Answering "What is your expected salary?"

| Situation | What to say |
|---|---|
| Asked very early, before you know the role | "I'd like to understand the responsibilities first. Could you share the budgeted range for this position?" |
| Pressed for a number | "Based on my research for this kind of role and my experience, I'm looking at [range] per month, depending on the overall package." |
| They share a range first | If it overlaps yours: "That works as a starting point; I'd expect to be toward the upper part given [reason]." If it is below: "That's below what I was expecting; is there flexibility for the right candidate?" |
| Asked for your current salary | Answer truthfully, or politely say you would rather focus on expectations for this role. Never inflate it; some employers ask for payslips or bank statements. |
| A remote foreign employer | Quote in their currency and their convention (monthly or annual), and confirm whether the role is employment or a contract |

Rules:
- Give a range, not a single number, and make the bottom of the range acceptable to you.
- Talk about the package: a lower base can make sense with strong benefits, and the reverse.
- Do not justify your number with personal expenses; justify it with the role, the market and your experience.

> **Fill in:** your range sentence, written out, for local roles and for remote roles.

## 3. Total compensation: what to compare

| Component | What to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Base salary | Gross or net? Monthly? When is it paid? | The foundation of everything else |
| Increments | When is the first review? How often? What decides the size? | A small base with a review after six months can beat a larger static one |
| Bonuses | Annual, performance or project bonuses? Guaranteed or discretionary? | Discretionary bonuses are not promises |
| Health cover | Outpatient and inpatient limits; family coverage; which hospitals | Large real value, often overlooked |
| Provident fund | Employer contribution, vesting rules | Long-term savings matched by the employer |
| Gratuity | Offered? After how long? | Paid on leaving, after a qualifying period |
| Statutory contributions | Deductions such as EOBI | Affects take-home pay |
| Allowances | Fuel, commute or pick-and-drop, internet or remote-work allowance, night shift, meals | Can add up to a meaningful share of the package |
| Leave | Annual, casual and sick days; public holidays; work-from-home days | Quality of life |
| Learning | Training budget, certifications, conferences | Growth you do not pay for yourself |
| Equipment | Laptop and monitor; who owns them | Comfort, and a cost you avoid |
| Overtime | Paid, compensated with leave, or neither? | Hidden hours change your effective pay |
| Tax | Income tax deducted at source | Compare take-home pay; tax slabs change with each federal budget, so use the current rates |

> **Fill in:** the full list of what your current package includes, so you can compare like for like.

## 4. Negotiating politely
Principles:
- **Wait for the written offer.** Negotiating in the first call, before they have decided they want you, weakens your position.
- **Be appreciative and specific.** Thank them, state the number or change you are asking for, and give one or two reasons (market data, the scope of the role, your directly relevant experience).
- **Negotiate the package.** If the base is fixed, other items often are not.
- **Do not bluff.** Mention another offer only if it exists, and be ready for them to say no.
- **Keep it short:** one or two rounds, then decide.
- **Get the final terms in writing** before you resign anywhere.

Scripts:
> "Thank you, I'm excited about this role. Based on my research and the scope we discussed, I was expecting something closer to [number]. Is there flexibility on the base salary?"

> "I understand the base is fixed. Could we look at [an earlier first review, a joining bonus, remote days, a learning budget]?"

> "I have another offer at [number]. I'd prefer to join you because [specific reason]. Is there a way to come closer to that?" (only if true)

> "Could I have until [date] to review the offer? I want to give you a considered answer."

What is often negotiable: base salary, joining date, the timing of the first salary review, title, work-from-home days, a joining bonus, learning budget, equipment, and sometimes probation length.

## 5. Evaluating an offer
Salary is one row in the decision, not the whole decision. Score each offer:

| Factor | Questions | Your weight (1 to 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Will I learn faster here? Is there a path to the next level? | |
| Stack | Is it the stack I want to go deep in (Rails, TypeScript, React Native, Django)? | |
| Mentorship | Are there senior engineers in my stack? Code reviews? | |
| Stability | Funding or client base, team turnover, how long the team has existed | |
| Probation | Length, terms, notice during probation, what confirmation depends on | |
| Notice period | After confirmation; could it trap you later? | |
| Contract type | Permanent, fixed-term or contractor | |
| Product or services | One product in depth, or many clients in breadth | |
| Hours and shift | Day or night, overtime culture, time-zone overlap | |
| Commute or remote | Time and cost of getting there, remote days | |
| Compensation | Take-home pay and benefits from section 3 | |
| Bond or penalty clauses | Any amount owed if you leave early? | |

> **Fill in:** your weights before you receive offers, so the decision is not driven by whichever number arrives first.

## 6. Local PKR roles vs remote foreign-currency contracts
Remote roles paid in a foreign currency can pay more in PKR terms, but the trade-offs are real. Treat everything here as general considerations; tax and banking rules change, so check current official guidance and consider professional advice.

| Aspect | Local role in PKR | Remote role in foreign currency |
|---|---|---|
| Legal status | Employee under Pakistani law | Often a contractor; sometimes employed through an employer-of-record service |
| Benefits | Health cover, provident fund, paid leave are common | Contractors usually get none unless the contract says so |
| Taxes | Deducted at source by the employer | You may be responsible yourself; special treatment of IT export earnings may apply depending on current rules and how the money is received |
| Payment | Monthly salary into your bank account | Bank transfer or an international payment service; check fees, conversion rates and payment delays |
| Currency | Fixed in PKR | Exchange-rate moves change your PKR income, in either direction |
| Contract law | Pakistani law, local norms | Often foreign law; disputes are hard to pursue |
| Job security | Notice periods protect both sides | Contracts can end with short notice |
| Hours | Local office hours or a defined shift | Overlap with the client's time zone, possibly late evenings |

Checklist for a remote contract:
- Who exactly is the contracting party, and are you an employee or a contractor?
- Currency, amount, and whether it is monthly, hourly or per milestone.
- Payment schedule, method, and who pays transfer fees.
- Invoicing requirements, if you are a contractor.
- Notice period for termination on both sides.
- Paid leave and public holidays (whose holidays?).
- Intellectual property assignment, confidentiality and non-compete clauses.
- Equipment: provided or your own?
- Required overlap hours.
- Governing law and how disputes are resolved.

> **Fill in:** whether you prefer local employment or remote contracting right now, and why (stability, benefits, income, learning).

## 7. Bonds and penalty clauses
Some employers include a service bond: you owe an amount if you leave before a set period, often tied to training. Before signing:
- Ask for the exact amount, the duration and what triggers it (resigning, termination, failing probation).
- Check whether it reduces over time.
- Get every term in writing, and for a large amount, consider legal advice.
- Weigh it against the offer: a bond limits your options if the role disappoints.

## 8. Resigning professionally
1. **Sign the new offer first.** Never resign on a verbal promise.
2. **Read your notice period** and any probation terms that still apply.
3. **Tell your manager first,** in a conversation, then send a short written resignation.
4. **Propose a handover:** a document of your ongoing work, credentials transferred properly, knowledge-transfer sessions, open tasks reassigned.
5. **Stay professional through the notice period:** finish what you can and keep your standards.
6. **Collect your documents:** experience letter, relieving letter, final settlement (salary, any leave encashment the policy allows), provident fund details.
7. **Return equipment** and remove your access to client systems as instructed.
8. **Counteroffers:** think about why you started looking; if the reasons were not only money, a raise rarely fixes them.

```text
Subject: Resignation - [Your Name]

Dear [Manager's name],

Please accept this as formal notice of my resignation from my position as
Associate Software Engineer. In line with my notice period, my last working
day will be [date].

Thank you for the opportunities and support during my time here. I will make
sure my work is handed over smoothly, and I am happy to help with the
transition in any way I can.

Kind regards,
[Your Name]
```

## 9. Offer-letter checklist
Before you sign, confirm in writing:
- [ ] Job title and level.
- [ ] Start date and work location (onsite, hybrid, remote).
- [ ] Base salary: gross or net, monthly or annual, payment date.
- [ ] Allowances (fuel, commute, internet, night shift, meals) and how they are paid.
- [ ] Bonus terms: guaranteed or discretionary, and when.
- [ ] Probation: length, terms, notice during probation, what confirmation depends on.
- [ ] Notice period after confirmation.
- [ ] Working hours, shift timings, overtime policy.
- [ ] Leave: annual, casual, sick, public holidays.
- [ ] Health insurance: coverage, limits, family members included.
- [ ] Provident fund, gratuity and statutory deductions.
- [ ] Increment and appraisal cycle, and the date of the first review.
- [ ] Bond, penalty, non-compete and non-solicitation clauses.
- [ ] Intellectual property and confidentiality terms.
- [ ] Equipment provided.
- [ ] Documents required for joining, and any background checks.
- [ ] Offer expiry date.

## 10. Tricky situations

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| The offer is below your walk-away number | Thank them, state the gap and your reasons once; if they cannot move and nothing else in the package compensates, decline politely and keep the door open |
| An "exploding" offer (decide by tomorrow) | Ask for a few more days; a reasonable employer agrees. Extreme pressure is itself information about the company |
| Two offers at once | Compare them with your section 5 weights, not just the salary; tell the company you prefer about the other offer only if it is real |
| They ask you to accept before sending anything in writing | Ask for the written offer first; never resign on a verbal promise |
| A company asks for your last payslip | Common practice for some employers; share it if you are comfortable, and never alter it |
| You already accepted, then a better offer arrives | Think hard: backing out damages trust, and the industry is small. If you do withdraw, do it quickly, honestly and politely |
| Salary paid late or partly in cash | A serious warning sign; ask how payroll works before joining |

## 11. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better |
|---|---|
| Naming a number without research | Build walk-away, target and stretch numbers first |
| Giving a single number | Give a range whose bottom you accept |
| Comparing gross base salaries only | Compare take-home pay plus benefits |
| Negotiating in the first screening call | Wait for the written offer |
| Inventing a competing offer | Use real market data and your value instead |
| Accepting on the spot out of relief | Ask for time to review, even a day |
| Ignoring probation, notice and bond clauses | Read them as carefully as the salary line |
| Resigning before signing | Sign the new offer, then resign |
